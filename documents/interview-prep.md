## 2. Product Strategy Questions

### Prioritization & Roadmapping

**Q1: How would you prioritize features for a new AI product?**
- **Framework:** RICE (Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort) weighted toward learning velocity for early-stage AI products
- **Jon's angle:** At Pottery Friends, I prioritized by killing scope ruthlessly — cut a planned marketplace after user interviews, pivoted the home screen when data contradicted the hypothesis, reduced 40 quests to 8. For AI specifically, I'd add an "uncertainty tax" to effort estimates and prioritize features where AI failure is recoverable (suggestions) over features where AI failure is catastrophic (automated actions).

**Q2: How would you decide which features to build with AI vs. traditional approaches?**
- **Framework:** Decision tree: (1) Is the task variable or deterministic? (2) Is the cost of error high or low? (3) Does the user expect personalization or consistency? AI wins on variable + low-error-cost + personalization.
- **Jon's angle:** At Swob, I challenged the founders' AI assumptions directly — their "AI-powered" candidate ranking was really a deterministic filter on availability and proximity. We reframed AI features as targeted automations, which was more honest and more shippable. At Macro Chef, AI meal planning was the right call because inputs are highly variable (macro targets, dietary restrictions, available ingredients) and the cost of a slightly imperfect recipe is low.

**Q3: You're the PM for a developer tool. How would you build the v1 roadmap?**
- **Framework:** Jobs-to-be-done mapped to developer workflow, starting with the "sharp edge" that causes the most friction
- **Jon's angle:** With Pottery Friends I built 15+ repos as a solo developer using AI tooling — I've lived the developer experience. For dev tools, I'd instrument the development workflow to find where developers lose flow state, then build the v1 around removing that single highest-friction moment. At Treasure DAO, I increased active developers 35% by shipping a developer portal that reduced data integration time 40% — the lesson is that developer adoption is gated by time-to-first-value.

**Q4: How would you think about pricing for an AI-powered product?**
- **Framework:** Value-based pricing anchored to the cost of the alternative (human labor, existing tools, manual process), not the cost of compute
- **Jon's angle:** At Frame Story, I built a 5-year financial model around freemium-to-paid conversion. For Pottery Friends, the strategy is free analytics dashboards as the wedge, then paid tiers for advanced features. AI products should price on the outcome (time saved, decisions improved) not the input (API calls, tokens consumed).

**Q5: How would you define success metrics for a product no one has built before?**
- **Framework:** Leading indicators over lagging — activation rate, time-to-value, and "would you be disappointed if this product disappeared?" (Sean Ellis PMF survey)
- **Jon's angle:** At Pottery Friends I'm running structured early access beta: tracking D1/D7 retention cohorts, activation funnels, and PMF surveys. The key insight is that in a new category, retention is more diagnostic than growth — if people keep coming back, you've found something; if they don't, no amount of acquisition fixes it.

**Q6: How do you balance platform vs. product thinking?**
- **Framework:** Start as a product (solve one user's problem completely), extract the platform when you see repeated patterns across users
- **Jon's angle:** At Mythical Games, I shipped a cross-title game services platform that unified engagement and monetization across multiple titles. The trick was that we started by deeply solving one game's needs, then identified the shared abstractions (player identity, live ops tooling, cross-title progression) that became platform capabilities. At Treasure DAO, I took the same approach with the developer platform.

**Q7: A competitor just launched an AI feature similar to what's on your roadmap. What do you do?**
- **Framework:** (1) Assess if their implementation actually solves the user problem or is just AI-washing. (2) Talk to users — did they switch? (3) Double down on your differentiated angle or leapfrog with deeper integration.
- **Jon's angle:** At Mythical Games, I defined the roadmap by identifying gaps competitors missed: live ops tooling, unified player identity, and cross-title progression. The best competitive response is rarely copying — it's finding the thing they can't build because of their architecture or business model.

**Q8: How would you evaluate whether to build, buy, or partner for an AI capability?**
- **Framework:** Build if it's core to your differentiation and you need to control the iteration cycle. Buy/partner if it's table stakes and your engineering time is better spent elsewhere.
- **Jon's angle:** At Pottery Friends, I built a custom CRM instead of using Mailchimp because tight integration mattered more than feature breadth. But for analytics, I used PostHog rather than building custom. For AI, I use Claude API directly rather than building my own models — the differentiation is in the product layer, not the model layer.

**Q9: How would you approach a new product line expansion?**
- **Framework:** Adjacent user, adjacent use case, or adjacent market — pick one axis of expansion at a time
- **Jon's angle:** Pottery Friends strategy is to start with pottery studios, then expand to woodworking, glassblowing, and other craft communities. The platform architecture supports this because the core problems (events, community knowledge, admin tools) are shared across craft verticals. At Zynga, I shipped across 4 franchises simultaneously, learning that shared systems enable efficient expansion.

**Q10: How do you handle technical debt in a fast-moving product?**
- **Framework:** Treat tech debt like a product decision — estimate the cost of not fixing it (velocity drag, bug rates, engineer morale) and prioritize against features using the same framework.
- **Jon's angle:** At Treasure DAO, I improved team delivery velocity by 25% partly by prioritizing a web app refactor. The refactored codebase then enabled faster shipping of the marketplace, testnet, and developer portal. The key is framing tech debt in terms interviewers/stakeholders care about: shipping speed, not code purity.

**Q11: How would you build a product strategy for an AI coding assistant?**
- **Framework:** Map the developer workflow end-to-end, identify where AI creates 10x leverage vs. marginal improvement, build around the 10x moments
- **Jon's angle:** I've built 15+ repos using Claude Code as my primary development tool. The biggest leverage isn't code generation — it's the ability to hold full project context and make architectural decisions. A coding assistant strategy should focus on reducing the gap between "what the PM/developer wants" and "working code," not just autocomplete.

**Q12: How do you think about AI product moats?**
- **Framework:** Data moats (proprietary training data), workflow moats (deep integration into daily habits), network moats (value increases with more users), and switching cost moats (accumulated customization)
- **Jon's angle:** At Pottery Friends, the moat isn't AI — it's community data. Glaze recipes, firing schedules, and technique knowledge that accumulate in the platform create switching costs no competitor can replicate on day one. For AI products, the model is commoditizing fast; the defensibility is in the product experience and proprietary data that feeds it.

## 3. Technical / AI Questions

### AI Product Development

**Q1: How do you evaluate whether an AI feature is actually working?**
- **Answer:** I measure on three axes: (1) Task completion rate — does the user accomplish what they set out to do? (2) User trust signals — do they edit the output, accept it, or abandon it? (3) Downstream behavior — does the AI feature change what users do next? At Macro Chef, I track whether users actually cook the AI-generated recipes (downstream behavior), not just whether they generate them (task completion). Acceptance rate without behavior change means the feature is entertaining, not useful.

**Q2: How do you measure AI product quality when outputs are non-deterministic?**
- **Answer:** You need a multi-layered evaluation: automated evals for regression detection (does the model still handle known edge cases?), human evaluation for subjective quality (does this meal plan look appetizing and nutritionally sound?), and user behavior metrics for real-world signal (did they use the output?). At Macro Chef, I built the Claude API integration with full context — macro targets, dietary restrictions, available ingredients, and recent meal history — because quality in AI products is largely a function of prompt quality and context richness.

**Q3: What's your experience with AI-assisted development?**
- **Answer:** I've built an entire product ecosystem solo using Claude Code: 15+ repos across React Native, Next.js, Supabase, Stripe, and PostHog. AI-assisted development doesn't just speed up coding — it fundamentally changes what a single PM can ship. I went from "I need an engineering team" to "I can validate a product thesis end-to-end in weeks." The key skill isn't prompting — it's knowing what to build and being able to evaluate whether the output is correct.

**Q4: How do you handle AI hallucinations in a production product?**
- **Answer:** Design the product so hallucinations are visible and recoverable, not hidden and dangerous. At Macro Chef, if the AI generates a recipe with incorrect macros, the user sees the calculated totals immediately and can regenerate. I also constrain the output space — structured JSON responses instead of free text, validated against known nutritional data ranges. The architecture question is: where does the AI output go before the user sees it, and what guardrails sit in between?

**Q5: How would you approach prompt engineering for a production AI feature?**
- **Answer:** Treat prompts like code: version them, test them against a regression suite, and review them in PRs. At Macro Chef, the meal planning prompt includes the user's full context (macro targets, dietary restrictions, available ingredients, recent meals) because specificity reduces hallucination. I chose Claude over GPT for longer context windows — the practical difference between a good and bad AI feature is often just how much relevant context you can fit in the prompt.

**Q6: How do you think about the UX of AI-powered features?**
- **Answer:** The core UX challenge is managing expectations around non-deterministic outputs. Users need to understand: (1) what the AI is doing, (2) how to steer it, and (3) what to do when it's wrong. At Macro Chef, I built three distinct modes (Quick Meal, Meal Prep, Pantry Raid) because the same AI capability serves different user contexts — Tuesday night "what can I make with what I have" is a fundamentally different interaction than Sunday afternoon "plan my week." Progressive disclosure of AI capabilities beats a single "do everything" input box.

**Q7: How do you decide between rule-based systems and ML/LLM approaches?**
- **Answer:** If the logic can be expressed as a decision tree and the inputs are structured, use rules. If the inputs are variable, the output requires creativity or synthesis, and approximate answers are acceptable, use AI. At Swob, I steered the founders away from ML-based candidate ranking because the actual decision was a simple filter (available Saturday night + within 5 miles). At Macro Chef, AI was the right choice because generating a recipe from macro targets, dietary constraints, and available ingredients requires creative synthesis no rule system can match.

**Q8: How would you build an AI feature roadmap?**
- **Answer:** Phase 1: AI-assisted (human in the loop, AI suggests, user decides). Phase 2: AI-automated (AI acts, user reviews). Phase 3: AI-autonomous (AI acts, user is notified). Each phase requires higher confidence in output quality and lower cost of error. You earn the right to automate by proving accuracy in the assisted phase. At Macro Chef, meal planning is Phase 1 — the AI generates, the user reviews and cooks. If accuracy data over time shows consistent quality, you can move to automated meal prep ordering (Phase 2).

**Q9: What technical tradeoffs do you consider when integrating an LLM API?**
- **Answer:** Latency (streaming vs. blocking), cost per call (model selection, prompt length), reliability (fallback behavior when the API is down), and context management (what goes in the prompt vs. what's retrieved). At Macro Chef, I chose Claude for longer context windows and use streaming responses so the user sees the recipe generating in real-time rather than waiting 3 seconds for a blank-then-complete experience. Cost management means being smart about what context is truly necessary vs. nice-to-have in each prompt.

**Q10: How do you think about data privacy and AI?**
- **Answer:** Three principles: (1) Minimize what you send to the model — only include user data that directly improves the output. (2) Be transparent with users about what data feeds the AI. (3) Never use AI outputs to make consequential decisions without human review. At Macro Chef, dietary and health data goes to Claude for meal planning but I don't persist conversation logs on Anthropic's side. For any AI product, the privacy architecture should be designed before the feature ships, not retrofitted.

**Q11: How do you evaluate competing AI models for a product feature?**
- **Answer:** Build the same feature against two models and measure: output quality on your specific task, latency at your expected scale, cost at your expected volume, and reliability over a week of production traffic. Benchmarks are useful for shortlisting but your specific use case is what matters. I chose Claude over GPT for Macro Chef because the meal planning prompt needed long context (full nutritional profile, pantry inventory, recent meals) and Claude's context handling produced more coherent recipes at that prompt length.

**Q12: How do you think about AI product safety?**
- **Answer:** Safety is a product design problem, not just a model problem. Define the blast radius of a wrong output — a bad recipe suggestion is annoying, a bad medical recommendation is dangerous. Build the product surface to match the risk level: high-risk outputs get human review gates, low-risk outputs can be fully automated. At the product level, this means constraining what the AI can do (output schemas, allowed action space) rather than hoping the model behaves.

## 4. Case Studies

### Case Study 1: Metrics & Analytics

**Prompt:** "You're the PM for an AI-powered code review tool. Define the success metrics framework and explain how you'd instrument the product."

**Approach:**
1. **North Star Metric:** Bugs caught before production (directly measures value delivered)
2. **Input Metrics:** Reviews completed per developer per day, median time from PR open to AI review delivered
3. **Quality Metrics:** Acceptance rate of AI suggestions (did the developer apply the fix?), false positive rate (suggestions dismissed), severity distribution of caught issues
4. **Business Metrics:** Developer time saved per week, reduction in production incidents, expansion rate (teams adopting after one team starts)
5. **Instrumentation:** Track every AI suggestion: what was suggested, was it accepted/modified/dismissed, and what happened downstream (did the code change make it to production? did it cause an incident?)

**Jon's edge:** "I built analytics pipelines from scratch at Big Fish Games (ETL + Tableau) and instrument everything with PostHog at Pottery Friends. I'd design the event taxonomy before writing the first line of product code — the instrumentation architecture is part of the product spec, not an afterthought."

---

### Case Study 2: Product Design

**Prompt:** "Design an AI-powered onboarding experience for a B2B developer platform."

**Approach:**
1. **User research:** Developers evaluate tools in the first 10 minutes. Time-to-first-value is everything.
2. **AI role:** Detect the developer's stack from their repo (language, framework, CI/CD), then auto-generate a getting-started guide customized to their environment — not a generic tutorial.
3. **Progressive disclosure:** Step 1: one-command install. Step 2: AI scans their codebase and suggests the highest-value first integration. Step 3: interactive tutorial personalized to their stack.
4. **Measuring success:** Activation rate (completed first meaningful action), D1 retention, time-to-first-value, and NPS at day 7.
5. **What to cut:** Don't show every feature. Don't require account creation before value. Don't make them read docs before trying the tool.

**Jon's edge:** "At Treasure DAO, I shipped a developer portal that increased active developers 35% by reducing integration time 40%. At Pottery Friends, I cut onboarding from 40 steps to 8 and completion jumped from 5% to 35%. The principle is the same: every step before the 'aha moment' is a reason to leave."

### Case Study 3: Prioritization

**Prompt:** "You have 5 feature requests from enterprise customers, 3 requests from your largest free-tier users, and your AI team wants to rebuild the model serving infrastructure. You have one quarter. What do you ship?"

**Approach:**
1. **Classify by urgency and impact:** Which enterprise requests are blocking expansion revenue? Which free-tier requests drive conversion to paid? Is the infrastructure rebuild enabling future velocity or just paying down debt?
2. **Apply RICE:** Score each on Reach (how many users affected), Impact (how much does it move the metric), Confidence (how sure are we about impact), Effort (eng weeks)
3. **Sequence:** Ship 1-2 enterprise features that unblock the most revenue first (quick wins build trust with sales). Allocate 30% of capacity to the infrastructure rebuild if it unlocks the AI team for the next 2 quarters. Defer free-tier requests that don't drive conversion.
4. **Communicate:** Create a transparent prioritization doc showing the tradeoffs. Let stakeholders see the math, not just the decision.

**Jon's edge:** "At Treasure DAO I defined the 2024 roadmap balancing marketplace, quest systems, developer platform, and a new chain launch — four competing workstreams with different stakeholders. I improved delivery velocity 25% by being transparent about tradeoffs and sequencing ruthlessly."

### Case Study 4: Go-to-Market

**Prompt:** "You're launching an AI writing assistant for technical documentation. Define the GTM strategy."

**Approach:**
1. **Target segment:** Developer-facing companies with 50-500 employees — large enough to have documentation pain, small enough that one champion can drive adoption.
2. **Wedge:** Free browser extension that improves existing docs (grammar, clarity, consistency) — zero commitment, immediate value. This is the "land."
3. **Expand:** Once a team is using the extension, offer the full platform: AI-generated API reference docs from code comments, changelog automation, docs-as-code CI integration.
4. **Channels:** Developer communities (Hacker News, Reddit, Dev.to), open source contributions with documentation (earn credibility), partnerships with API-first companies who need better docs.
5. **Pricing:** Free for individuals, team tier at $15/seat/month, enterprise with SSO and custom model training.

**Jon's edge:** "At Frame Story, I designed the phased GTM for Cluck: build an owned audience through email capture before launch, rather than depending on platform algorithms. At Genies, I defined the GTM strategy that grew the alpha community to 1K users. The pattern is always: lead with free value, capture the relationship, then expand."

---

### Case Study 5: Technical Tradeoff

**Prompt:** "Your AI feature uses a large language model and costs $0.15 per request. Usage is growing 20% month-over-month. The CFO wants to cut AI costs by 50%. What do you do?"

**Approach:**
1. **Understand the value chain:** Which AI calls generate the most user value? Which are commoditized? Rank by value-per-dollar.
2. **Technical levers:** (a) Prompt optimization — shorter prompts with same quality reduces token cost. (b) Model tiering — use a smaller/cheaper model for simple tasks, reserve the expensive model for complex ones. (c) Caching — if 30% of queries are similar, cache responses. (d) Batching — aggregate non-urgent requests.
3. **Product levers:** (a) Rate limiting for free tier. (b) Usage-based pricing that passes cost to high-volume users. (c) Cut AI features that users don't actually value (measure acceptance rates).
4. **What NOT to do:** Don't degrade quality for paying users. Don't remove the AI feature that drives conversion. Don't optimize cost without understanding which costs create value.

**Jon's edge:** "At Macro Chef I chose Claude over GPT partly for cost efficiency at my prompt length. I also designed three meal planning modes (Quick Meal, Meal Prep, Pantry Raid) that vary in prompt complexity — not every interaction needs the full context window. This is the same principle: match the cost to the complexity of the task."

## 5. Questions Jon Should Ask

### Product Culture & Process

1. **"How does your team decide what NOT to build?"** — Signals that you know shipping less, better, is harder than shipping more. Reveals their prioritization discipline.

2. **"Walk me through the last feature you killed after it was already in development."** — Tests whether they have the culture to cut losses. If they can't name one, that's a red flag.

3. **"How do PMs and engineers share ownership of technical decisions?"** — Reveals whether PMs have real influence on architecture or are just writing tickets.

4. **"What does your product review process look like? Who's in the room?"** — Shows you care about decision-making structure, not just building.

### AI Strategy

5. **"Where in your product does AI create genuine 10x value vs. where is it a marginal improvement?"** — Forces the interviewer to be honest about AI-washing vs. real AI leverage.

6. **"How do you evaluate AI feature quality internally before shipping to users?"** — Tests their eval infrastructure maturity. Many companies ship AI features without systematic evaluation.

7. **"What's your approach to model selection and when do you switch between models?"** — Signals you understand the practical tradeoffs of model cost, latency, and capability.

8. **"How does your team handle the non-deterministic nature of AI outputs in QA and testing?"** — A deeply practical question that reveals engineering maturity.

### Team & Leadership

9. **"What does the PM own end-to-end here vs. what requires committee approval?"** — Directly assesses your autonomy. Senior PMs need real ownership to do their best work.

10. **"How do you think about the balance between platform/infrastructure investment and user-facing features?"** — Reveals their time horizon and technical debt philosophy.

11. **"What's the biggest product bet you've made in the last year that didn't work? What did you learn?"** — Tests psychological safety. If leadership can't discuss failures openly, the culture won't support good product work.

12. **"How does the team handle it when user research contradicts the CEO's vision?"** — Tests whether data actually drives decisions or just decorates them.

### Growth & Trajectory

13. **"What would success look like for this role at 90 days, 6 months, and 1 year?"** — Shows you think in milestones and want clear accountability.

14. **"What's the biggest organizational bottleneck slowing down shipping velocity right now?"** — Signals you're already thinking about unblocking the team, not just building features.

15. **"Where do you see the biggest growth opportunity in the next 12 months — and what could derail it?"** — Shows strategic thinking and awareness that every opportunity has risks.
