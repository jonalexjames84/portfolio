## 1. Zero-to-One / Building from Scratch

### "The First PM at Genies"

**Situation:** Genies was an 85-person startup with zero product infrastructure. No PM had ever been hired. Celebrity partnerships with Gucci and GIPHY were in motion but there was no product framework to execute against.

**Task:** As the founding Product Manager, I needed to build the entire product function from scratch: define the creator ecosystem vision, stand up product processes, and ship an e-commerce storefront and mobile app while managing high-profile partnerships.

**Action:** Built the Creator Ecosystem from concept to launch. Defined GTM strategy that grew the alpha community to 1K users. Launched an e-commerce storefront and shipped a mobile app with GIPHY integration. Worked directly with celebrity partners to translate brand requirements into product specs.

**Result:** E-commerce storefront generated $100K in weekly sales. Mobile app scaled to 3K early users. The creator ecosystem became the product story that helped secure $150M in funding.

**Answers:** "Tell me about a time you built something from nothing." / "Describe a time you had to create structure where none existed." / "How do you handle ambiguity?"
**Best audience:** Startup (Seed to Series B)

### "One PM, Fifteen Repos"

**Situation:** After being laid off in November 2024, I identified an unowned vertical: no one had built purpose-built software for craft studio communities. Pottery studios were running on Square + paper binders + group texts. The ops manager at my local studio was spending 5+ hours per week on manual admin.

**Task:** Validate the market thesis and build a full product ecosystem solo — native mobile app, marketing site, analytics dashboards, and internal docs — to prove that modern AI tooling changes what a single builder can do.

**Action:** Embedded at Red Ox Ceramics 6 days a week for a year to understand the problem deeply. Conducted 20+ user interviews. Built the entire platform using AI-assisted development with Claude Code: 15+ repos, 59 database migrations, 19 edge functions across React Native, Next.js, Supabase, Stripe, and PostHog. Prioritized ruthlessly — cut a planned marketplace after user interviews revealed studio owners saw it as competition, pivoted the home screen when engagement data contradicted my hypothesis, reduced 40 onboarding quests to 8 tied to real studio actions.

**Result:** 150 beta members with consistent weekly usage. Gamification completion rates jumped from 5% to 35% after the quest redesign. Home screen redesign (feed-first to events-first) doubled engagement. Currently running structured early access beta tracking D1/D7 retention cohorts and PMF surveys.

**Answers:** "Tell me about a time you built a product from scratch." / "How do you validate product-market fit?" / "Give me an example of using data to make a hard call." / "How do you work with AI tools?"
**Best audience:** Startup, growth-stage tech company

### "The PAC-MAN Pivot"

**Situation:** At Bandai Namco, I managed product operations for the PAC-MAN franchise at global launch scale — 10M+ weekly installs, 1M+ MAU. The team was relying on organic acquisition as the primary growth channel, but retention was underperforming expectations.

**Task:** Diagnose why retention was lagging despite massive install volume and recommend a strategy shift.

**Action:** Analyzed cohort data across acquisition channels and discovered that organically acquired users had significantly lower intent and engagement than paid users. This was counterintuitive — the assumption was that organic users were higher quality. I built the case for pivoting acquisition spend toward paid channels with better targeting, presented the data to leadership, and led the transition.

**Result:** Retention improved 20% because paid users had higher intent. The insight reshaped how the team thought about acquisition quality versus volume — it is not about how many users you get, it is about getting the right users.

**Answers:** "Tell me about a time data changed your strategy." / "Describe a counterintuitive insight you uncovered." / "How do you approach user acquisition?" / "Tell me about a time you influenced a team to change direction."
**Best audience:** Growth company, enterprise with data-driven culture

## 5. Data-Driven Decisions

### "The Feed-First Failure"

**Situation:** When building Pottery Friends, I designed the home screen around a social feed — an Instagram-style experience where members would share photos of their work. The hypothesis was that community content would drive daily engagement.

**Task:** Validate or invalidate the home screen hypothesis using real user data, and act on whatever the data showed.

**Action:** Launched the feed-first home screen to the beta community and instrumented everything with PostHog. Tracked where users actually went, what they tapped, and how long they spent on each screen. The data was unambiguous: members skipped the feed and went straight to Events. They came to the app to find out what was happening at the studio, not to scroll content.

**Result:** Killed the feed-first home screen and redesigned around upcoming events and quick actions. Engagement doubled. This became a core lesson I reference constantly: ship your hypothesis, measure it, and kill it fast if the data says no.

**Answers:** "Tell me about a time you were wrong." / "How do you use data to make product decisions?" / "Describe killing a feature you believed in." / "How do you validate product hypotheses?"
**Best audience:** Any — universally resonant

---

### "Swob's 3x Onboarding Drop-Off"

**Situation:** While consulting for Swob (swipe-to-hire job platform), I set up PostHog analytics to track conversion at every funnel stage. The data revealed a 3x drop-off at onboarding — the original flow asked for business details, team size, and scheduling preferences before users ever experienced the product.

**Task:** Diagnose the onboarding problem and redesign the flow to reduce friction while still collecting necessary business information.

**Action:** Reduced the onboarding form to just email + business name, then collected additional information progressively after users had their first "aha moment." Also reframed the founders' AI feature assumptions: challenged predictive turnover models (not enough data, legal exposure) and repositioned "AI" features as well-designed automations.

**Result:** Significant onboarding conversion improvement. Strategic questioning saved months of misallocated engineering effort on AI features that were really automations. Swipe-to-match prototype tested with 3 restaurant managers: average time to shortlist dropped from days to under 2 minutes.

**Answers:** "Tell me about a time you improved a key metric." / "How do you approach onboarding design?" / "Describe a time you challenged assumptions." / "How do you balance data with intuition?"
**Best audience:** Growth company, SaaS

### "The Marketplace That Never Was"

**Situation:** While building Pottery Friends, I had planned a marketplace feature where studio members could sell their work directly through the app. It seemed like a natural extension of the community platform.

**Task:** Validate whether a marketplace was the right next feature to build for studio communities.

**Action:** Conducted user interviews with studio owners and discovered that they saw an in-app marketplace as direct competition with their own retail operations. The owners, who were my primary customers and the ones who would pay for the platform, did not want their members selling ceramics through a channel the studio didn't control. Instead, what studio owners actually wanted to pay for was analytics dashboards showing member engagement, retention, and event attendance.

**Result:** Cut the marketplace entirely and redirected engineering effort into analytics dashboards — the feature owners actually wanted to pay for. This became the foundation of the freemium business model: free for events and messaging, paid for analytics, payments, and gamification.

**Answers:** "Tell me about a time you killed a feature." / "How do you prioritize what to build?" / "Describe a time user research changed your plan." / "How do you find product-market fit?"
**Best audience:** Startup, product-led growth company

I shipped Macro Chef, a health analytics platform. I consulted for Swob. I attended the Berkeley AI conference and immersed myself in AI-native product development. In 5 months, I shipped more than most PMs ship in 2 years at a company.

A portfolio that demonstrates I can conceive, design, build, and ship without a team, a salary, or a safety net. The mental health journey — lifting, nutrition, fighting through anxiety — made me a better builder, not just a healthier person.

**Answers:** "Tell me about a time you faced adversity." / "How do you handle setbacks?" / "What did you do during your career gap?" / "What motivates you?"

## 10. Failure / Learning

### "The Six-Source Integration Mistake"

**Situation:** When building Macro Chef (health analytics dashboard), I tried to auto-import data from 6 different sources: Strava, DEXA, COROS, Apple Health, Garmin, and MacroFactor. Each API had different auth flows, rate limits, and data formats.

**Task:** Build integrations that would unify all fitness data into one dashboard.

**Action:** Attempted to build all 6 integrations simultaneously. Quickly discovered that the integration complexity was unsustainable — each new source added exponential testing surface and maintenance burden.

**Result:** Cut to 3 core sources (Strava, DEXA, COROS) that covered 90% of the value with 50% of the effort. The lesson: scope ruthlessly around the Pareto principle. The best product decisions are about what you cut, not what you add.

**Answers:** "Tell me about a time you over-scoped." / "How do you decide what to cut?" / "Describe a technical decision you reversed." / "What have you learned about scope management?"
**Best audience:** Any — shows self-awareness

---

### "The White-Label Trap"

**Situation:** While consulting for Swob, the founders wanted to build a white-label theming system — 8 themes, 7 layouts — to support B2B restaurant partnerships. They built it before talking to a single B2B partner about what they actually needed.

**Task:** Advise on whether the white-label system was the right investment of engineering resources before fundraising.

**Action:** Challenged the founders to validate the distribution thesis before building premium infrastructure. When early customers finally engaged, they only wanted their logo and brand color — not 8 themes and 7 layouts.

**Result:** Advised cutting the white-label scope dramatically. The lesson I surfaced for the team: validate distribution thesis before building premium infrastructure. Every feature built before talking to customers is a bet, and most bets lose.

**Answers:** "Tell me about a time you gave hard feedback." / "How do you prevent wasted engineering effort?" / "Describe a time you challenged a stakeholder's assumptions." / "What is your approach to build vs. validate?"
**Best audience:** Startup, SaaS

### "The Monolith That Overwhelmed"

**Situation:** Swob's first version had all 5 features (hiring, pipeline, shifts, candidate dashboard, admin) behind tabs in a single monolithic app.

**Task:** Understand why user testing showed managers were overwhelmed and recommend a fix.

**Action:** Observed user testing sessions where restaurant managers could not find what they needed. Diagnosed the problem: different personas (manager, candidate, admin) have different mental models and workflow needs. Recommended splitting into 5 separate apps, each with a focused UI and independent deployment cycle.

**Result:** Separate apps solved the cognitive overload problem. Each app could serve as its own investor demo. The lesson: personas need separate products, not separate tabs.

**Answers:** "Tell me about a time you simplified a product." / "How do you approach information architecture?" / "Describe a design decision informed by user testing." / "What have you learned about product complexity?"
**Best audience:** Growth company, SaaS, enterprise

### "The Quest Overload"

**Situation:** When building Pottery Friends, I launched with 40 gamification quests to drive onboarding engagement — thinking that more content would mean more activation.

**Task:** Understand why gamification completion rates were stuck at 5% despite having abundant content.

**Action:** Analyzed completion data and discovered that 40 quests created decision paralysis, not engagement. Most quests were disconnected from actual studio actions. Cut from 40 to 8 quests, each tied to a real studio action (attend an event, post a photo, try a glaze from the library).

**Result:** Completion rates jumped from 5% to 35% — a 7x improvement. The lesson: activation is not about volume of content, it is about connecting each step to real value the user can see immediately.

**Answers:** "Tell me about a time you simplified to improve results." / "How do you approach onboarding and activation?" / "Describe a time less was more." / "How do you use data to iterate on a feature?"
**Best audience:** Any — consumer, SaaS, growth
