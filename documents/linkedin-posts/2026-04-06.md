## POST 1 — Monday, April 6
**Type: Hot Take**

---

PMs who can't prompt are the new PMs who can't read a SQL query.

At Berkeley ProductCon last month, an interviewer mentioned they now ask candidates: "What's in your prompt library?"

Not "have you used AI?" Everyone has used AI. The question is whether you've built systems around it.

In 2015, the move was SQL literacy. The PM who could pull their own data didn't wait three days for an analyst. They moved faster, asked better questions, earned more credibility with engineers. By 2020, that was table stakes.

Today the move is prompting fluency. Not because AI does the thinking — it doesn't. But because the PM who can write a context-rich brief that gets usable output on the first pass, run parallel agent workflows to compress a week of discovery into a day, and build a Claude project loaded with company goals and product principles...

That PM operates at a different speed than one who doesn't.

I've spent 15 years watching the speed gap separate good PMs from great ones. At Jam City, the teams running currency optimizations weekly instead of quarterly were the ones compounding growth — 20% month-over-month. Speed of iteration beat any single brilliant idea.

AI prompting fluency is the new speed gap. The tools exist. The question is whether you're building deliberate practice around them, or treating them like a fancy autocomplete.

What's in your prompt library?

## POST 2 — Wednesday, April 8
**Type: Here's What I Built**

---

I got laid off in November 2024. By February 2025, I had a production app live with 150 beta members.

No co-founder. No engineering team. No funding.

Just Claude Code and 15 years of knowing what a product actually needs.

Pottery Friends is a craft studio community app. Here's what "solo with AI" actually looks like in practice:

- Native mobile app (React Native)
- Marketing site (Next.js)
- Analytics dashboards (PostHog)
- Payment processing (Stripe)
- 15+ repos, 59 migrations, 19 edge functions
- Structured beta tracking D1/D7 retention cohorts, activation funnels, PMF surveys

Production features shipped in days, not sprints.

But the AI didn't do the product thinking. I still had to:
- Kill the marketplace feature after user interviews revealed nobody wanted to buy from strangers
- Rebuild the home screen when engagement data showed users were skipping the feed (engagement doubled after the redesign)
- Cut 40 onboarding quests to 8 after completion stalled at 5% (it jumped to 35%)

That's the actual lesson from AI-native development: the bottleneck moves from engineering to decision-making. You ship faster, which means you learn faster, which means the quality of your hypotheses matters more — not less.

AI replaced the team I didn't have. The judgment? That's still the job.

Curious what other PMs are building solo with AI tools. What's your stack?

## POST 3 — Friday, April 10
**Type: Framework / Lesson from 15 Years**

---

After 15 years of prioritization frameworks, I've boiled it down to one question.

Not "what's the ROI?" Not RICE. Not ICE. Not "what does the data say?"

The question: What's the fastest path to learning something we don't know?

Here's why it beats everything else.

At Jam City, 20% month-over-month revenue growth had us convinced we were doing the right things. The question should have been: what don't we understand about why this is working? When we dug into that, it changed our entire monetization model.

At Pottery Friends, I spent two weeks designing a marketplace feature. Then I talked to 8 users. Nobody wanted to buy from strangers in their local craft community. I killed it before writing a line of code. The fastest path to learning wasn't a build — it was a conversation.

The problem with most prioritization: it optimizes for execution rather than learning. Teams pick the thing they're most confident about, which is usually the thing they already know how to build. Your highest-confidence bets are often your lowest-information ones — you're confident because you haven't stress-tested them yet.

Every item on a roadmap should answer two things:
1. What will we learn from shipping this?
2. Is that the most important thing to learn right now?

If you can't answer both, it shouldn't be at the top of your list.

Discovery and delivery aren't separate phases. They're the same loop, running faster.

What's the question you ask before committing to anything on the roadmap?
