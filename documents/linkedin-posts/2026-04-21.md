## POST 1 — Monday, April 21
**Type: Hot Take**

---

The PM job description hasn't changed. The PM job has.

At the Berkeley AI Conference, Perplexity's VP of Growth said something I've been thinking about since: "We're seeing a separation where engineers and designers are actually making more product decisions. So the PM role becomes: can you see the larger picture? Can you be the director of the movie?"

Here's what I think is actually happening:

The PM role is splitting into two distinct archetypes.

The first is the technical PM who lives close to the model — tuning prompts, designing eval frameworks, running quality experiments. A PM in title, closer to an ML engineer in practice.

The second is the strategic PM who operates at the system level — owning the narrative, bridging technical and commercial, making calls with incomplete data. They're the one who decides which feature to kill, when to pivot, how to frame a tradeoff for leadership.

Both are called "product manager." They require completely different skills.

Neither path is wrong. But the worst place to be is the middle: the generalist PM who does a little of everything while waiting for someone else to define what the role means at AI-native companies.

My read: if you're technical, go deep on evals and model behavior. If you're strategic, build your track record of shipping things without a full engineering team. Either way, the bar for what PMs should demonstrate has permanently moved up. Apple's head of product said it plainly: "Download Cursor, toss your resume in, and build a resume website. That's an awesome first step."

Pick a lane. Go hard.

Which type of PM are you becoming?

## POST 2 — Wednesday, April 23
**Type: Here's What I Built**

---

"Can you build something we can show investors next week?"

That was the brief when I started fractional PM work for Swob, a pre-funding AI hiring platform. No dedicated design team. No engineering bandwidth for prototypes. Just me, Claude Code, and a stack I know well.

Five working prototypes in two weeks. Each one used directly in investor meetings and enterprise sales conversations.

Here's what "AI-native prototyping" actually looks like:

The founders had been calling the product "AI-powered." When I dug in, most of it was automation, not intelligence. I reframed their roadmap around targeted automations with real impact — which cut months of misallocated engineering time and surfaced legal risks in their predictive turnover model before those became a deal-breaker with enterprise buyers.

Then I built. Claude Code + Next.js + Supabase. Each prototype served two purposes simultaneously: investor demo and living engineering spec. When the engineers came on, they had something to implement rather than a deck full of wireframes.

The standout: an AI-ranked candidate pipeline aggregating Indeed, ZipRecruiter, and native applicants. Swipe-to-match interface. Manager shortlisting time went from days to under 2 minutes.

The pattern I keep seeing — with Swob, with Pottery Friends, with every solo build I've shipped this year — is that AI tools have fundamentally changed what one person with PM judgment can do. Speed is no longer the constraint.

Knowing what to build and what to kill before you write a line of code — that's still the job.

## POST 3 — Friday, April 25
**Type: Framework / Lesson from 15 Years**

---

I shipped a home screen. The data told me it was wrong. I killed it.

That sequence — ship, measure, kill, rebuild — is the core of good product development. Most teams skip the middle part.

Here's what happened with Pottery Friends:

I built a feed-first home screen for a craft studio community app. The hypothesis: people want to see what's happening in their community when they open the app.

The reality: users opened the app, skimmed the feed, and went straight to the events section. Every time. The feed had engagement in aggregate. It had almost none as an entry point.

So I killed it. Rebuilt the home screen around events. Engagement doubled.

The hypothesis wasn't wrong because I'm bad at product. It was wrong because hypotheses are usually wrong until they aren't. That's the point of shipping.

The lesson I've carried across 15 years: the longer you protect a hypothesis from data, the more it costs you when you finally let the data in. The teams I saw do this well — at Jam City scaling to 1M+ DAU, at AAA launching to 6M members — ran tight feedback loops and treated every shipped feature as a test, not a conclusion.

AI tools compress this loop further. You can ship in days instead of weeks, get data in days instead of months. The time between "this is our hypothesis" and "here's what users actually do" shrinks to almost nothing.

But only if you're willing to look at the data. And then act on it.

What's a hypothesis you've had to kill based on what users actually did?
