## POST 1 — Monday, April 28
**Type: Hot Take**

---

Micromanagers might finally have their moment.

Box's CTO said something that stopped me at the Berkeley AI Conference: managing AI agents is "weirdly similar" to managing humans. Give them vague instructions, you get vague output. Give them sophisticated context, you get sophisticated results. Same as any direct report you've ever had.

He also said micromanaging agents is a feature, not a bug. You can be as detailed, as demanding, as specific as you want — and the agent just does the work. No passive-aggressive Slack messages. No one-on-ones to repair the relationship. Just output.

Here's what that means for PMs:

If you've spent years writing context-rich briefs, decomposing ambiguous problems into concrete tasks, and evaluating whether output matches intent — you are already better at agent management than most engineers.

This isn't prompt engineering. It's management fundamentals: clear objectives, relevant context, explicit success criteria. The same things that made you effective with people make you effective with agents.

The engineers Haines described aren't writing code anymore. They arrive at work, kick off 10–20 agents simultaneously, then spend the day evaluating output. Cancel this branch. Revert that one. This one's good — keep going.

That's a PM workflow. We've just been running it with humans.

The PMs who build this skill now will operate at 5–10x the throughput of those who don't. The window for reps is open. The question is whether you're building them deliberately.

What does your agent management practice look like? Same as directing a direct report, or something different?

## POST 2 — Wednesday, April 30
**Type: Here's What I Built**

---

I co-founded a game studio with no legal background, no CFO, and no fundraising experience.

Here's what zero-to-one actually looked like:

Frame Story is a collaborative game studio where small creative pods own their IP and earn 70% of net revenue. The studio handles ops, legal, finance, and publishing so creators can focus on making great games.

Building it meant owning everything I'd normally hand off:

- Incorporated the LLC, set up banking and payments, drafted contributor agreements and IP assignments from scratch
- Built a 5-year financial model: 1-to-12 title portfolio strategy, revenue share modeling, burn projections
- Structured a $3M cap SAFE raise — targeting $100K pre-seed across SAFEs, grants (Epic MegaGrant, Wings Fund), and crowdfunding
- Designed the full GTM pipeline for our first title: Steam Next Fest demos, indie festival strategy, creator partnerships, wishlist-to-funding milestone map

None of this is what I did as a PM for 15 years. But the skills transfer exactly: scope decisions under uncertainty, model assumptions explicitly, define success metrics, build toward a milestone.

AI tooling compressed the ramp. Financial models that would've taken two weeks took two days. First drafts of investor memos, legal structure outlines, cap table scenarios. I still had a lawyer review everything and an accountant validate the model. But I came to those conversations prepared instead of starting from zero.

The real lesson from building Frame Story: zero-to-one isn't a product skill. It's a systems skill. You have to build the entire system — legal, financial, operational, GTM — before the product work even starts.

## POST 3 — Friday, May 2
**Type: Framework / Lesson from 15 Years**

---

Most enterprise AI pilots are failing right now.

Box's CTO said so at the Berkeley AI Conference. Then he said the more important thing: "Before you read too far into that — what's the base rate of success of large projects? Most technology projects fail."

The base rate for enterprise technology initiatives — ERP rollouts, cloud migrations, CRM implementations — has held at 50–70% failure for decades. AI projects failing at similar rates isn't evidence that AI is broken. It's evidence that organizations are organizations. Same change management problems. Same undefined success metrics. Same scope creep.

The question isn't whether the first attempt fails. It's what happens when they try again.

Here's the framework I'd use as a PM leading any AI initiative:

**1. Design the pilot for learning, not ROI.** What will you learn about your data quality? Your users' adoption curve? Where the model actually struggles? That signal is worth more than any incremental efficiency the pilot was supposed to deliver.

**2. Plan the second attempt before the first one ships.** Document what you expect to learn and what you'd change in v2. It reframes a "failed pilot" as "phase 1 of a two-phase implementation." The organizational narrative matters.

**3. Move from pilot to production faster than feels comfortable.** The window between successful pilot and company-wide rollout is shorter in AI than in any previous technology wave. If you're still in pilots when competitors are in production, you've lost the rep advantage.

The biggest risk isn't a failed pilot. It's using a failed pilot as a reason to stop.

How are you framing AI initiative failures with your leadership team?
